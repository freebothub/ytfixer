YouTube Reused Content Appeal Script: What to Say (and What Not to Say)

Guide · Updated November 2026 · About 8 min read · by the YTFixer Team

YouTube gave you 21 days to appeal your reused content rejection, and the appeal is a video — not a form, not an email. That is good news and bad news: good, because a reviewer can actually see how you make your videos; bad, because five minutes on camera is enough time to talk yourself out of an approval. This guide gives you a structure and example wording to adapt to your own channel — not a script to copy, because a made-up story is worse than an honest one. Use the structure, fill it with your own facts, and show your real work. Nothing here guarantees approval — only YouTube decides.

What a video appeal actually is

When YouTube rejects a channel for reused content, you have 21 days to appeal. The appeal is a short video, under five minutes, in which you appear on camera and explain your channel. You must say or show your channel URL in the first 30 seconds, so the reviewer knows the video is about your channel. The reviewer looks at your channel as it stands now — not as it was when you applied, and not as you promise it will be. That is the whole point: your chance to show, not just claim, that your content is original. If the appeal fails, you cannot appeal the same rejection again — you would wait 90 days and reapply. So only appeal if the channel would pass a fresh review today. If it still needs real changes, fix it first and reapply after 30 days from a first rejection. A failed appeal costs you the shorter clock.

Fix the channel before you record a word

An appeal video cannot fix a channel that is still full of reused content — reviewers check the channel, not just the video. So before you write a single line, do the fix: delete or set to private any pure re-uploads and compilations with almost no added value, and remake the risky videos with real commentary, your own footage, or a new structure — or remove them if you cannot remake them properly. Then publish several new videos where your face, voice, script, and editing are obvious from the first ten seconds. While you do this, record short clips of yourself scripting, filming, and editing, and save drafts and timelines — that footage is the evidence your appeal needs. Our full guide to fixing a reused content rejection walks through this audit.

The five-part structure that works

Keep your appeal under five minutes. Use these five parts, in this order:

  1. Introduce yourself and your channel (first 30 seconds): your name, channel name, and channel URL, spoken and shown on screen.
  2. Say what happened, in one sentence: “My channel was rejected for reused content on [date].” Do not spend a minute on the backstory.
  3. Show how you make a video. The longest part, about two minutes: your script, your filming, your editing timeline.
  4. Walk through two or three of your most original videos. For each, say what you made yourself and what you added to any material you did not film.
  5. Say what changed and close, in one or two sentences about what you removed or remade.

Practice with a timer. If you run over five minutes, cut words from part 3, not parts 1 or 4.

What to say in the first 30 seconds

The first 30 seconds prove the video is yours and tell the reviewer what to look for. Do not open with an apology or your channel’s history. Say something like this, in your own words:

“Hi, my name is [your name], and this is my channel [channel name] — youtube.com/@[your handle], shown on screen now. I am appealing the reused content rejection from [date], and here I will show you how I make my videos and what I have changed.”

That is about 20 seconds — your name, your channel, and your URL in the first 30 seconds. Then move straight to your process.

How to show your creation process on camera

Telling a reviewer you make original videos is weak. Showing them is strong — aim for about two minutes of process footage, narrated by you as it plays. Start with the script: show the document where you planned the video. Then show yourself filming — a short clip of you on camera, or your camera set up while you record. Then show the edit: record your screen as you open your editing project, so the reviewer can see the timeline — your voice track, your cuts, your text, your graphics. Point at what you did: “Here I cut the original clip to twelve seconds and added my own explanation over it.” Use only real footage of your own work — if you edit on your phone, show your phone. A simple honest process beats a grand invented one. Keep each clip to ten or twenty seconds.

Example wording for your own videos

For each example video, follow the same pattern: what the video is, what you made yourself, and what you added to anything you did not film. Here is the shape of it:

“In this video, [your video title], I wrote the script myself and filmed [what you filmed]. The clip at [time] comes from [source]; I used twelve seconds of it to show [what it shows], and over it I added my commentary explaining [your point]. After it, I [what you did next — your test, your opinion, your example].”

Be honest about what is not yours. If a video uses someone else’s clips, say so plainly and show what you added. Permission from the original creator is worth mentioning, but it is not the main point — permission answers the copyright question, not the reused content question. What the reviewer needs to hear is what you added: your words, your analysis, your footage, your examples.

What never to say

Leave all of these out:

  • “I had permission to use the clips.” Permission does not fix reused content. Spend your time on what you added.
  • Any complaint about YouTube or the reviewer. Saying the system is unfair does not help your case.
  • “Other channels do the same thing and they are monetized.” Comparisons do not change your channel.
  • “I promise I will make original videos from now on.” Promises are not evidence.
  • A script you copied from the internet. If the words do not match the channel, the reviewer will notice.
  • Anything about buying subscribers, views, or watch time. Buying engagement breaks YouTube’s policies and can end a channel.
  • Begging, threats, or emotional pressure. A calm, factual video is far more persuasive.

If a sentence does not show your process or your changes, cut it.

Step-by-step: recording and submitting your appeal

  1. Write bullet points, not a full script. A word-for-word script sounds read aloud, and bullet points keep you natural.
  2. Record in a quiet room with your face clearly visible. You must appear on camera yourself.
  3. Keep it under five minutes. Practise with a timer and cut anything that does not show your process or your changes.
  4. Put your channel URL on screen in the first 30 seconds, and say it out loud.
  5. Upload the video as unlisted. It does not need to be public, only watchable by the reviewer.
  6. Submit the video through the appeal link in YouTube Studio or in your rejection email, and check the link works first.
  7. Wait. Do not reapply while your appeal is being reviewed, and do not send the same appeal twice.

Checklist: is your appeal ready?

  • Channel fixed first: risky videos removed or remade, new original videos published.
  • Your face and voice are in the video, and your channel URL appears in the first 30 seconds.
  • Video is under five minutes, practised with a timer.
  • You show your real process: script, filming, editing timeline.
  • You walk through two or three of your most original videos.
  • You say what you added to any material you did not film yourself.
  • No copied script, no complaints, no promises instead of evidence.
  • Video uploaded as unlisted and the link tested before submitting.
  • You know your dates: appeal within 21 days; if it fails, reapply after 90 days.

Frequently asked questions

How long should my appeal video be?

Under five minutes — about four minutes is a good target. Practise with a timer.

Can I appeal if I have not fixed my channel yet?

No — fix the channel first. The reviewer looks at your channel as it stands now, so an appeal video cannot cover up videos that are still reused content. If you appeal too early and it fails, you must wait 90 days to reapply instead of 30.

Do I need to show my face in the appeal video?

Yes. You must appear on camera and speak in the video yourself, with your channel URL in the first 30 seconds. Showing your face while you walk through your process is the strongest evidence that the work is yours.

What happens if my appeal is rejected?

You cannot appeal the same rejection again. You can reapply 90 days after the rejection — use that time to keep fixing the channel, and reapply only when it would pass a fresh review.

What to do next

Start with the fix, not the camera. Audit your videos, remove or remake the risky ones, publish a few that are unmistakably yours, and save clips of yourself making them. Then write your bullet points, record a practice run, and check it against the checklist above. If you want a second pair of eyes before you use your one appeal, an expert review can tell you whether the channel is ready. No audit can guarantee YouTube will say yes, but an honest appeal that shows real work gives you the best chance you have.
