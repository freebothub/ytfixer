YouTube Copyright Claim vs Strike: What Each One Means

Policy guide · Updated 8 November 2026 · 8 min read · by the YTFixer Team

Open YouTube Studio one morning and there it is: a notice about copyright on one of your videos. Is your channel in danger? Will you lose monetization? Before you panic, delete the video, or pay a stranger who promises to fix it, read the notice carefully. The most important question is which kind of notice you have received.

A claim and a strike sound similar, but they are not the same. One usually changes a single video. The other is a formal warning on your channel record, and three in 90 days can terminate it. This guide explains the difference and what to do next. Only YouTube decides.

What is a Content ID claim?

A Content ID claim is usually automatic. YouTube’s Content ID system compares audio and video in uploads against a catalogue of material submitted by rights owners. When it finds a match, the rights owner’s chosen action is applied to that video. No human reviewer has necessarily looked at it, and no formal legal takedown has necessarily been filed.

A claim usually affects that one video. The rights owner may monetize it, block it in some regions, or track its viewing figures. In many cases the video stays up, but the advertising revenue goes to the rights owner while the claim is active. That is frustrating, but it is not a penalty on your channel.

Most importantly, a Content ID claim is not a strike. It does not count against your channel standing on its own. A channel can have claims and still apply for the Partner Program, if the channel as a whole meets the program’s policies.

What is a copyright strike?

A copyright strike follows a formal legal takedown request from a rights owner. The video is removed from YouTube, and the strike is recorded against your channel. This is not an automatic matching notice. It is a formal complaint that your video infringed copyright, and YouTube has acted on it by taking the video down.

A strike counts against your channel. Three strikes in 90 days can terminate your channel. One strike is serious. Two strikes leave you with very little room for error. Three in the same 90-day period can end the channel altogether, including its videos, subscribers, and its place in the Partner Program if it was monetized.

Strikes expire in 90 days if you complete Copyright School and receive no more strikes in that period. Until then, understand what caused the strike, remove the cause, and do not repeat it. In YouTube Studio you can wait, ask for a retraction, or submit a counter-notification if the removal was mistaken.

Why the confusion matters

There is another confusion that causes just as much harm. Copyright and reused content are separate systems. Copyright asks whether you have the legal right to use the material. Reused content asks whether your channel’s content is meaningfully yours. You can have full permission and still be rejected for reused content, because permission solves the copyright question and does not make the content yours.

What to do when you get a Content ID claim: step by step

  1. Read the notice in YouTube Studio. Note what was matched, where, and who claimed it.
  2. Decide what the material is. Music, a clip, or background audio? A few seconds, or most of the video?
  3. Check your rights first. Do you have a licence or written permission? Find the document before you dispute.
  4. Choose one honest option. If you have the rights, dispute briefly. If not, trim the part, replace the audio, or remove the video.
  5. Do not dispute out of anger. A dispute says you have a valid basis. Disputing a correct claim fixes nothing.
  6. Check the whole channel. Search your other videos for the same music, clips, or source.

What to do when you get a copyright strike: step by step

  1. Read the takedown notice in full. Note who made the request, what video was removed, and why.
  2. Do not re-upload the removed video. Re-uploading removed content is a fast way to collect another strike.
  3. Do not open a new channel to dodge the strike. Work the official process on the channel that received it.
  4. Complete Copyright School when asked. Strikes expire in 90 days if you complete it and get no more strikes.
  5. Consider a retraction request. If the takedown was a mistake, ask the claimant for a retraction in YouTube Studio.
  6. Consider a counter-notification only if it is true. Use it only when the removal was mistaken or you have the rights.
  7. Audit the rest of the channel the same day. Check every other video for the same source or music, and fix the risk first.

Common mistakes that make things worse

First, paying a stranger who promises to remove strikes instantly. No one outside YouTube can remove a strike instantly. The real routes are the official ones in YouTube Studio. Never share your Google password with anyone.

Checklist: before you dispute a claim or respond to a strike

  • I have read the full notice in YouTube Studio, not just a summary.
  • I know what was matched or removed, and whether this is a claim or a strike.
  • I have my licence, permission, or other evidence ready, if I will rely on it.
  • I have checked my other videos for the same music, clip, or source.
  • I have not re-uploaded removed content or opened a new channel to avoid a strike.
  • I have not shared my password, and I have not paid anyone who promised an instant removal.
  • I understand three strikes in 90 days can terminate the channel, and strikes expire in 90 days if I complete Copyright School and get no more.

How claims and strikes affect monetization

For the YouTube Partner Program, the current thresholds are 1,000 subscribers and 4,000 valid public watch hours in 12 months, or 10 million valid public Shorts views in 90 days. From 1 February 2027, new applicants will need 8,000 hours or 20 million Shorts views instead.

A claim on its own does not stop you applying, but a claimed video may not earn revenue while the claim is active, and a channel full of claimed material may fail the reused content test. A strike is a mark on your channel’s record. Sort out claims and strikes first, then apply with a channel you can explain honestly. That will not guarantee acceptance. Only YouTube decides.

Frequently asked questions

Is a Content ID claim the same as a copyright strike?

No. A claim is usually an automatic match and affects only that video. A strike is a formal takedown request, the video is removed, and the strike counts against your channel. Three strikes in 90 days can terminate the channel.

Can a claim turn into a strike?

They are different processes, but the same material can lead to a strike if the rights owner files a formal takedown request. Fix the cause of a claim, not just the single video.

Do strikes expire?

Yes. Strikes expire in 90 days if you complete Copyright School and receive no more strikes. Use that time to audit the channel and remove the cause.

Should I delete a video with a claim?

Not automatically. If you have a valid basis, dispute with evidence. If not, trim the claimed part, replace the audio, or remove the video. Delete it if it is mainly someone else’s material and you added little to it.

What to do next

Start with the notice itself. Open YouTube Studio, read it in full, and write down in one sentence whether you have a claim or a strike, what was matched or removed, and who claimed it. Then work the step-by-step list for your situation, and fix the habit, not just the video.
